# 28 March 2026

*Burrow, Saturday morning, before anyone else is up*

We came down for Mum Weasley's birthday yesterday and stayed over. I'm sat at the kitchen table with a cup of tea that's gone cold because I keep forgetting to drink it, and the garden is absolutely full of gnomes again — Arthur has given up and says they're "ecological," which is what he says about anything he can't be bothered to deal with. The chickens are arguing about something. I can hear Ginny and her mum moving about upstairs.

A lot has happened.

The review board ruled in my favour on Wednesday. Completely. Dawlish's complaint was dismissed and there was even a mild reprimand attached to it for "procedural misuse," which in the Ministry means *somebody tried to weaponise this and got caught*. Robards shook my hand afterwards and said "that's that, then," in the tone he uses when he very much hopes that is not, in fact, that.

But here's the strange part.

I went home that night and told Ginny, and instead of feeling vindicated I felt — tired. Properly tired. The way I felt after the Battle, sort of, except without the grief. Just emptied out. And I realised I'd been bracing myself for weeks, maybe months, for this fight, and now that I'd won it I didn't actually want the thing I'd been fighting for. I didn't want to go back to my desk on Monday and do it all over again in six months when someone else decided I was a problem to be managed.

So on Thursday morning I went to see Hermione.

I told her I wanted to talk about the Academy post she's been dangling at me since 2023. I told her I wasn't promising anything yet. I said I wanted to see what it actually looked like, the shape of the days, whether I'd still get to do real casework as an instructor or whether it was going to be all paperwork and ceremony. She listened the way she does, where you can see her making three lists at once behind her eyes, and then she said, "Harry, I've been waiting for this conversation for so long I genuinely don't know what to say."

And then she hugged me, which she almost never does at work.

I haven't told anyone else yet. Not Ron, not the kids. Ginny knows, obviously. She's being very careful not to seem pleased, which is how I know she's extremely pleased. She keeps touching my shoulder when she walks past me, little squeezes, as though she's checking I'm still there.

I'm going to take the rest of the weekend and not think about it. Fred and George's boys are coming over later for a Quidditch kickabout — George has three now, and they play the way their father and uncle played, which is to say as if the rules were a suggestion offered by someone they didn't respect. Lily will want to keep up with them and probably will. Ron has promised to referee and will definitely cheat.

I looked at my reflection this morning in the window over the sink — it's got warped somehow over the years, all the Burrow windows have — and I looked like Dad. Not for the first time. But for the first time in a while I thought: *good*. He was thirty-eight when he died. I've had seven years he didn't get. I should spend them better than sitting in a review hearing.

The kettle's going again. Someone's up.

— H
